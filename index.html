Mini investment memo · pre-seed · Warsaw, PL

Sunbay.io — automation platform for finance & ops teams

Ask: $1.2M at $6M post Our model check: $500k (8.3%) Fund: $70M Deck: 2025 (traction to Apr-25) Memo: Nov 2026 Author: Andrea Schenini

Sections A–C judge the company strictly on the deck we were sent and on third-party data available to any analyst at the time (market, competitors, benchmarks). The company has since raised and repositioned; that is covered separately in the post-script so it does not contaminate the core judgement. Every external figure carries a numbered source.

A · Top recommendation

Pass at these terms

PASSconviction: high

Sunbay is pitching a generalist "automate anything, one use case at a time" platform while the only thing customers actually use is invoice-collection reminders, and the company's own numbers show that: collections were 70–90% of automated actions every month, and services revenue ($3k) was roughly double software MRR ($1.2–1.5k). That is a consulting shop with a product attached, not yet a software company. The team is committed but incomplete: two non-technical founders are full-time and the CTO is "full-time soon", so we cannot tell who builds the product or how defensible it is against a finance team armed with Zapier, their invoicing SaaS, or an LLM. The returns picture follows from that: our hurdle for a $500k pre-seed cheque is 5x, ideally 10x, and only the upside scenario (a ~$100M exit, roughly 8x) clears it, while the company the deck actually describes lands in the 0.2–2x range. Probability-weighted, the expected return is around 4x, but with a 40% chance of losing money and almost half of that expectation resting on a 5% outlier scenario; strip the outlier and it is about 2.5x. There is a real, boring pain here and a plausible niche business, but at $6M post on this positioning it is not a venture bet we should make.

B · Key arguments & risks

Why one could invest, and why we shouldn't

The case for investing

  • The pain is real and recurring. Intrum estimates 11% of European business revenue is paid late every year, and its 2024 report found firms spend 73 working days a year chasing payments[18]. Nobody enjoys this work, so retention of a tool that removes it should be strong.
  • There is a pricing gap in the mid-market. AR specialists start at $259/month (Chaser) and $650 per user/month (Kolleno) and are UK/US-centric[11][13]; Polish SMEs with 10–200 staff and local ERPs (Symfonia, Comarch, enova) are under-served. Local integrations are a moat Anglo players will not build.
  • The data already tells them where to focus. Collections generated 254, 259, 638 and 464 automated actions in Jan–Apr 2025 versus 2–144 for the other modules[1]. A team that follows this signal has a clear vertical product.
  • Founders are full-time, resourceful and revenue-generating from month one. Even services revenue at pre-seed shows they can sell, and the deck is unusually honest about what is "done", "soon" and "under development".
  • Entry price is low by market standards. Carta's median post-money cap for US rounds of $1–2.5M was about $15M in 2025[9]; $6M post gives us 2.5x more ownership per dollar than a comparable US deal.
  • Upside case. Sunbay drops the platform story, becomes "the Chaser of CEE" for invoice-heavy SMEs, reaches ~€15M ARR in 6–7 years on the back of local ERP integrations and payment-behaviour data, and is acquired by an ERP vendor or an AR consolidator (Billtrust/HighRadius type) at 7–8x revenue: a ~$100–120M exit, 8–10x on our cheque. That clears our hurdle comfortably; the question is how likely it is from this starting point, not whether it would be big enough.

Key risks and mitigations

  • Services creep. Consulting already outweighs software 2:1; the "future paths" slide lists "bootstrap with a product & services mix" as a live option[1]. Mitigation: tranche the cheque; release the second half only when software MRR ≥ 2x services revenue for three consecutive months.
  • No full-time technical founder. Nikodem is "full-time [SOON]"; two ops/finance founders cannot build a defensible software product alone. Mitigation: closing condition that the CTO is full-time on a standard 4-year vest before money moves, plus technical due diligence on what exists today (own code vs. no-code).
  • Generalist positioning dilutes everything. "Being generalist is in our blood" is a sales line, not a strategy; the Uber city-by-city analogy ignores that Uber dominated one city first. Mitigation: board-level agreement on a collections-only roadmap for 12 months with named KPIs (DSO reduction, invoices under management).
  • Build-vs-buy and incumbent feature creep. Polish invoicing SaaS already ships automatic email/SMS payment reminders[17]; Zapier/Make cost tens of euros; an LLM plus a spreadsheet replicates templated chasing in an afternoon. Mitigation: defensibility must come from deep ERP connectors, dispute/promise-to-pay workflow and payment-behaviour data, none of which is in the deck today.
  • Unit economics unknown at a $250/month price point. At ~$3k ACV, outbound SMB sales in Poland is hard to make pay back inside 12 months. Mitigation: demand CAC, payback and gross-margin data before any term sheet; insist on a product-led or partner-led (accountants, ERP resellers) channel.
  • Downside case. The company settles into a €300–500k/year automation agency with a thin product layer, never raises a priced seed, and our 8% becomes illiquid equity in a lifestyle business: 0–0.5x recovery via a small acquihire, more likely a write-off after 3–4 years of management attention.
C · Section analyses · 1

Team

  • Filip (CEO) is the right profile to sell and fundraise, with a caveat. Nine years in ops and automation and a stint at YC-backed Plane (payroll for startups, SF)[19] means he has seen a venture-grade GTM machine from inside. But the pedigree is as an employee, not a founder, and the "try to apply to YC until they accept us" line suggests at least one rejection already.
  • Dawid (COO) is the operator the business currently runs on. Five years in finance and operations at Droppe, a Finnish industrial B2B marketplace that raised €3.9M in 2024[20], maps directly to the buyer persona (finance specialist at a 50–200 FTE company). He is the one closest to the customer's pain and probably the one delivering the consulting revenue.
  • Nikodem (CTO) is the hole in the team. Five years of software engineering at mBank and startups is adequate, but "full-time [SOON]" at a company that has already shipped a product raises the obvious question: who wrote the current product, and who owns it? Either the two business founders are building on no-code tools, or the code is partly the work of a part-timer with a day job. Both are weak foundations for the "platform" the deck promises.
  • Judgement signals cut both ways. Credit: both business founders went full-time in April–May 2025, and the deck is honest about status. Debit: a Hannah Montana lyric as the "solution" slide and a Witcher screenshot for strategic options read as a team optimising for being memorable in a demo day crowd rather than for a serious investor conversation. In a pre-seed where we are buying judgement, that matters.
  • The founders themselves are unsure this is a venture business. Their listed options include "fail and starve", "bootstrap with a product & services mix" and "raise an angel round to prove the VC path is for us"[1]. We would be funding an experiment on whether they want to be VC-backed. That is a fine thing for angels to do; it is not what a $70M fund's reserves are for.
C · Section analyses · 2

Product

  • One feature is live; two are promises. Bulk email/SMS payment reminders with templates work; the ElevenLabs-powered voice agent is "launching soon" and AI legal-document generation is "under development"[1]. The honest reading of slide 5 is: a reminders tool with a nice dashboard, plus OCR and bulk invoice generation as utilities.
  • The "one set of features, many use cases" architecture is platform thinking before product-market fit. Slide 6 treats reminders as Lego bricks reusable for document requests and contract renewals. Those are different buyers (legal, sales ops) with different triggers and systems of record; sharing a notification engine does not make them one product. This is how a small team ends up with three half-products.
  • What the deck shows is replicable; what would be defensible is absent. Templated reminders are built into Polish invoicing SaaS (Fakturownia sends SMS/email before, on and after the due date)[17] and into Xero/QuickBooks. A finance lead with ChatGPT or Claude and Zapier can wire the same flow. Real AR moats are ERP-depth integrations, dispute and promise-to-pay handling, escalation to legal/debt registers, and payment-prediction data. None appears in the deck.
  • The dashboard mock hints at the right product, which the narrative then abandons. The Collection Flow screen (status, invoice, step, delay in days, risk flags) is a credible AR workflow UI. If the pitch had been "this screen, for every invoice-heavy SME in Poland, integrated with Symfonia and Comarch", we would be having a different conversation.
  • "Toyota Corolla, not Ferrari" is the wrong metaphor for a pre-seed. Reliable and versatile is what you become after you have won a niche. At this stage investors want a sharp, narrow tool that a specific buyer cannot live without. The deck proudly describes the opposite.
C · Section analyses · 3

Market, with a bottom-up TAM

  • Top-down, this is a mid-sized, mid-growth category. IMARC sizes global AR automation at $3.0B in 2025, reaching $6.9B by 2034, a 9.3% CAGR[8]. That is solid, not explosive: no tailwind strong enough to carry a generalist to a large outcome on its own.
  • Bottom-up, Poland alone caps out around €50M of serviceable revenue. GUS counts 2.37M non-financial enterprises in 2024, of which 2.0% are small (10–49) and 0.6% medium (50–249), about 62k firms in the 10–249 band[6]. Filtering for invoice-heavy B2B sectors and real collection pain, and pricing at Sunbay's own later list price, gives a Polish SAM of roughly €50M (model below). A venture outcome needs the EU, where the same logic yields ~€1.5B, and where the incumbents already sit.
  • The deck has no market slide at all. No TAM, no segment choice, no geography. Slide 2 names three personas (real estate, renewables, IT equipment) that have little in common except "sends many invoices", which is a hint that the real segmentation variable is invoice volume, not industry. The deck never says so.
  • Why-now is generic. "AI is becoming mainstream" is true of every pitch in 2025. The specific why-now for AR, that cheap LLMs now make tone-adjusted, multilingual, negotiation-capable chasing feasible at SME prices, is a good argument the deck does not make.
  • Late payment is a European structural problem, which helps the long-term thesis. Intrum's 2025 report (9,150 executives, 25 countries) estimates 11% of revenues paid late and warns that up to 10M European businesses face closure risk within two years[18]. The demand side is not the problem; the product focus and the price point are.

C · Section analyses · 4

Competitive landscape & differentiation

  • The deck's competitor slide names four products and analyses none. Tesorio and Collbox are dismissed as "$1k+/month for large volumes", Zapier and Make as "require setup". Missing entirely: the SMB-priced AR specialists (Chaser from $259/mo, Kolleno from $650/user/mo, Upflow with a free tier)[11][13][15], the Polish invoicing platforms with built-in reminders, and the elephant: ChatGPT/Claude as a do-it-yourself substitute.
  • The funded specialists are small, which cuts both ways. Chaser (London, 2013) has raised roughly $5–6M in total[12]; Kolleno (London, 2020) raised a £4M seed[14]; Tesorio (US) is the exception at $37.5M[16]. Good news: no one has locked up European SMEs. Bad news: a decade of AR startups has not produced a breakout, which says something about the category's ceiling.
  • Differentiation as pitched is "cheaper and simpler", which is not a moat. Price is replicable and simplicity erodes with every added use case. The only durable differentiators available here are local ERP integrations (Symfonia, Comarch Optima, enova365, wFirma), Polish legal escalation (KRD debt register, interest notes under Polish law) and collection-outcome data. The deck mentions none of them.
  • The real competitor is "do nothing" plus a spreadsheet. Slide 3 says so itself ("hire more people and build bigger spreadsheets"). Winning that fight needs a 10-minute onboarding from the customer's invoicing system, not a "platform". The 2026 live product's one-click import and €1,000 implementation fee[4] suggest they learned this.
  • Horizontal automation tools are not the threat the deck thinks. Zapier/Make are the alternative for tech-savvy users, but the real risk is the opposite end: LLM agents making bespoke workflow automation trivial for everyone, which commoditises exactly the generalist "automate the boring" layer Sunbay wants to own, while leaving vertical AR workflow (integrations, legal, data) intact.

C · Section analyses · 5

Traction & benchmarking

  • Revenue says consulting, not SaaS. April 2025: $1.2–1.5k MRR from 5–6 clients plus ~$3k consulting[1]. Software is about one third of revenue, and ~$250 per client per month is a price at which a direct sales motion cannot pay back. This is the single most important slide in the deck and it confirms the thesis of the whole memo.
  • The north-star metric is the wrong one, and it dipped. "Automated actions" counts emails sent, which is an input, not an outcome; customers buy days-sales-outstanding reduction and cash collected. The metric also fell from 735 to 664 total actions between March and April, with collections dropping 638 → 464, in the very month the deck was being written.
  • Module usage is the clearest product signal in the deck and the team ignores it. Collections were 92%, 85%, 87% and 70% of all automated actions from January to April[1]. Customers vote with usage; the roadmap (voice agents, legal docs, contract renewals) votes elsewhere.
  • Against pre-seed benchmarks, the round is too large for the proof. Carta's median US cap for $1–2.5M rounds is ~$15M[9], so $6M post is cheap on paper, but the $1.2M ask implies ~370x trailing software ARR. In our experience European pre-seeds of this size either show €10k+ software MRR with month-on-month growth or a team with a prior exit. Sunbay shows neither.
  • The path to a priced seed is steep from here. To reach the ~€25k software MRR a seed investor would expect within 18 months, Sunbay needs roughly 18% compound monthly growth from $1.35k, or 50+ paying customers at the deck-era price. The calculator below lets you test alternatives.

C · Section analyses · 6

The round & returns

  • Terms: $1.2M at $6M post is 20% to new money, the top of the usual 10–20% pre-seed band. Our $500k would buy 8.3%, enough to lead or co-lead with a board observer seat, pro-rata rights, 1x non-participating preference and information rights. We would push for a tranche structure (see Risks) rather than a lower price; the price is not the problem.
  • What "success" means for a $500k pre-seed cheque in a $70M fund. We underwrite early-stage positions to a 5x hurdle (roughly 30% IRR over 6–7 years) and want a credible path to 10x, because a 30–35 position portfolio needs its winners to carry the losses. We do not need this one deal to return the fund; we need its upside to be large and reasonably likely. The simulator below is built around those two thresholds.
  • Dilution path matters more than entry price. Two further priced rounds at 20% each plus a 10% option pool takes 8.3% to ~4.8% at exit; three rounds takes it to ~3.8%. At 3.8%, 5x needs a ~$65M exit (≈$10M ARR at 6–7x) and 10x needs ~$130M. Those are the numbers to hold in mind while reading the deck's $1.35k MRR.
  • Scenario returns. The company the deck describes (services mix, no priced seed) returns 0.2–0.5x. A disciplined niche Polish AR SaaS sold to a local acquirer returns ~2x. The "Chaser of CEE" upside, a €15M ARR business bought by an ERP vendor or AR consolidator at 7x, returns ~8x and ~35% IRR. A pan-European category leader would return 30x+, but no European AR company has reached that scale in a decade of trying. Weighting those at 40/35/20/5 gives an expected ~4.3x, just under the hurdle, but the shape is the problem: only a 25% chance of clearing 5x, a 40% chance of a loss, and almost half the expected value sitting in the 5% outlier. Without the outlier the expectation is ~2.5x.
  • What would have to be true for this to clear the hurdle. (1) Sunbay narrows to collections and reaches €1M software ARR within 24 months at ≥80% software mix; (2) ERP integrations create switching costs that lift net revenue retention above 110%; (3) a European AR consolidator or ERP vendor pays 7x+ revenue in 2031–32; (4) we deploy reserves pro-rata through Series A to hold ~6%. Each is plausible alone; together they describe a company the deck does not present. The pass is about probability, not about the size of the prize.
  • Our conclusion on the round. This is an angel or micro-fund cheque (€100–250k from operators who can help with Polish mid-market distribution), where a 2–3x expected outcome on a small ticket is fine. For us, at $500k, we would stay close and reconsider at seed if software MRR exceeds €25k with services under 20% of revenue: at that point the upside scenario becomes the base case and the hurdle math works.

Post-script · outside the deck

What actually happened, and whether it changes the call

In June 2026 Sunbay announced €550k from Kogito Ventures with s20 and angels including Kahoot's CTO[2][3]. That is less than half the $1.2M asked in this deck, which is consistent with the market pricing the generalist story down, or with the founders cutting the round to what they could close. More importantly, the company we see today is not the company in the deck.

The deck (2025)

  • "The automation platform for finance & operations teams"; "being generalist is in our blood"
  • Collections as use case 1 of many; document requests and contract renewals next
  • ~$250/month per client; 5–6 clients; $3k consulting on top
  • No competitor analysis, no market sizing, no integrations named

The live product (Nov 2026)

  • "Accounts receivable automation platform. Build, run and automate your entire collections process."[5]
  • Explicitly not for "a few invoices a month"; targets wholesale, logistics, subscription, manufacturing, real estate
  • €290/€490/€690 per month at 250 invoices, €1,000 implementation; 20+ businesses in 5 countries[4][2]
  • Integrations with Symfonia, enova365, Comarch, SAP B1, Dynamics, Xero, QuickBooks, wFirma, inFakt; KRD legal escalation; AI query agent[5]

Every move they made is the move this memo asks for: vertical focus on collections, 2x the price point, local ERP integrations as the moat, legal escalation as the Polish-specific feature. That validates the diagnosis and says something good about the founders' ability to listen to their own data. It does not change the recommendation on this deck at these terms, because we would have been paying $6M post for the generalist company and hoping for the vertical one. It does move Sunbay onto the watch list: at 20 customers on €490/month the software run-rate is still only around €10k MRR, so the seed-round test we set above (€25k software MRR, services under 20%) is the right trigger to re-engage.

Lesson for our process: when usage data in a deck contradicts the narrative, underwrite the usage. Here it predicted the pivot 12 months early.
